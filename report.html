Interpretation: appearance, economic loss and perceived value in "The Necklace"

7.1 Research paradigm and what the figures can and cannot show

This study follows a reader-oriented, phenomenographic approach. Phenomenography describes qualitatively different ways of experiencing a phenomenon (Marton, 1981), while reader-response theory treats meaning as something realised through reading rather than simply stored in a text (Iser, 1978). My initial impression, written after reading three sampled blocks, was the starting point for the dimensions, research question and extraction prompt. It was not a hypothesis to be proved by software.

The computational steps follow the spirit of algorithmic criticism (Ramsay, 2011): the machine reorganises the text so that patterns can be compared and discussed. The counts below describe where the coded vocabulary appears. They do not establish what the story means; each numerical pattern needs to be interpreted through close reading.

7.2 Summary of the evidence

7.3 Dimension 1: class and social aspiration

The opening block establishes a gap between Mathilde's circumstances and the life she imagines she deserves. She is born into "a family of clerks" and has "no dowry, no expectations, no means" of being recognised by a wealthy man. The contrast is not only between poverty and wealth; it is also between her sense of beauty and the social position available to her.

The descriptions of luxury make that imagined life concrete: "Oriental tapestries", "vast living rooms", "rare old silks", "priceless ornaments" and "fine dinners". Against these scenes, the "dirty walls", "worn-out chairs" and "ugly curtains" of her home become signs of a life she resents. The contrast helps explain why the invitation to the ball matters so much: it offers Mathilde a brief chance to appear to belong to the world she desires.

7.4 Dimension 2: appearance and identity

Mathilde's appearance is closely connected to how she imagines herself and how she wants to be seen. The story describes her as "pretty and charming", with "beauty, grace, and natural charm". Yet beauty does not free her from social limits. Her plain dress and lack of jewels make her feel excluded from the world in which she hopes to be admired.

The borrowed necklace changes her appearance at the ball. For one evening, Mathilde receives the attention she wants. After the necklace disappears, however, adornment becomes tied to a lasting material consequence. The replacement necklace allows her to conceal the loss, but the debt transforms her daily life for ten years.

7.5 Dimension 3: debt and material loss

The missing necklace changes the story from social aspiration to material crisis. In the middle block, the couple search "in the folds of her dress, in the folds of her cloak, in her pockets, everywhere", but cannot find it. Their uncertainty gives way to the decision to replace the necklace. In the final block Mathilde explains that the replacement "has taken us ten years to pay for".

The debt is not just an amount of money. It reorganises the couple's lives and becomes a long period of work and hardship. The final conversation reveals that Mathilde has carried this burden because she believed the original necklace was valuable. Madame Forestier's statement that it was "an imitation" worth "five hundred francs at most" makes the scale of the sacrifice newly visible.

7.6 Dimension 4: irony and false value

The ending depends on the difference between what the characters believe about the necklace and what it is actually worth. Mathilde and her husband treat the missing necklace as a valuable object and spend years replacing it. Madame Forestier's final revelation—that the original was imitation—reverses the meaning of that sacrifice.

The story also builds the reversal through similarity. Mathilde says she returned "another exactly like it" and that the two necklaces were "very similar". Those phrases make appearance a source of confidence while also preparing the reader to question whether appearance reliably indicates value.

7.7 Limitations

The method depends on a word list. Words such as "wealth", "beautiful", "necklace" and "imitation" can have different meanings in different passages, while matching counts them according to the selected phrases. Some relevant language may also be absent from the extraction.

The extraction was generated from a prompt and then checked against the story. Phrase selection remains a judgement that another reader or model could make differently.

The division into segments and stages is interpretive. Different paragraph breaks or scene boundaries could change stage-level rates. Short segments can also produce high densities from a small number of matches.

Counts show coded vocabulary, not tone or meaning. Irony, social pressure and emotional change are only partly represented by phrase matching.

7.8 Conclusion

In "The Necklace", Mathilde's desire for a wealthier life makes appearance and possession feel closely tied to social value. The lost necklace turns that desire into years of debt, and the final revelation separates the necklace's apparent value from its actual price. The close reading suggests that the ending's force comes from this gap between appearance, belief and cost. The figures help trace where the relevant language appears; interpretation explains why those patterns matter.

Method note

Phrases were extracted by Claude (Anthropic), claude.ai chat using a prompt written by the researcher, checked against the story, and matched case-insensitively as whole words, with longer phrases matched first and overlapping text counted once. The statistical tests are descriptive; phrase hits within a passage are not independent.
